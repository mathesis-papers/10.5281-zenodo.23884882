OEIS A164660, final FORMULA (29 May 2016): Chebyshev numerators and floor products

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

The manuscript asks why a numerator obtained by integrating a polynomial agrees with a product built from rounding down integer powers. Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang prove that these constructions always give the same sign: positive precisely when the integer parameter is divisible by four. They also show that changing the product’s exponent or translating its interval often leaves its value unchanged.

Chebyshev polynomials have a useful defining property: evaluated at the cosine of an angle, the polynomial of degree m gives the cosine of m times that angle. Call it Tₘ and integrate it from zero to one. Integrating its integer coefficients produces a rational number; reduce the fraction and retain its signed numerator. Wolfdieter Lang introduced the associated sequences in 2009. Anthony Browne’s conjecture of 29 May 2016 concerns degree n+1 for every positive integer n, linking this integral construction to divisibility.

The authors first evaluate the whole fraction. Degrees zero and one give 1 and 1/2. For even m≥2, the integral is −1/(m²−1); for m congruent to one modulo four it is 1/(m+1); for m congruent to three modulo four it is −1/(m−1). Each fraction is already reduced, so this supplies the complete denominator sequence too. Substituting a cosine for the integration variable gives a multiple-angle cosine multiplied by a sine from the change of variable. A product-to-sum identity reduces the integral to endpoint cosine values, whose fourfold repetition explains the sign pattern. This also proves the preceding rational-integral conjecture in the sequence entry.

The floor product rests on an elementary detector. The floor of a number is the greatest integer not exceeding it. For integer t, the floors of t/n and (t−1)/n differ by one exactly when n divides t, and otherwise agree. Thus each factor in Browne’s product is minus one when n divides kⁿ, and plus one otherwise, for k from one to n. Define rad(n) as the product of n’s distinct prime divisors, with rad(1)=1. Exactly n/rad(n) factors are negative, so the product is minus one raised to this count. This recovers Browne’s earlier floor-sum formula; n=1 is checked separately.

More generally, replace the exponent by any positive integer r. If a prime appears e times in n, it must appear at least the ceiling of e/r times in k for n to divide kʳ; the ceiling rounds upward. Multiply the required prime powers to obtain a divisor D of n. Qualifying integers are precisely multiples of D, with n/D in every block of n consecutive integers, including blocks crossing zero. This proves invariance under integer translations. For n>1, the count equals n/rad(n) exactly when r is at least the largest prime exponent in n, sharpening a sufficient bound credited to Bottomley.

Counts can change without changing signs. For n=16, exponent two requires multiples of four, giving four qualifying residues; exponent four requires even integers, giving eight. Both products are positive. Only powers of two affect parity: for r≥2, D removes n’s sole factor of two, but never all its factors of two when there are at least two. Thus n/D is even exactly when four divides n, giving the integral numerator. Exponent one always gives minus one, so n=4 shows the restriction is necessary. The authors credit earlier square-, cube-, and fourth-power counts and S. Fried’s 2025 proof of a related conjecture of Yanev.

The authors also correct a factor-of-two discrepancy in a coefficient-table rule and a sign error: the relevant integrated coefficient of the degree-seven polynomial is −56/3, not 56/3. These follow from integrating individual coefficients, not approximation. Summing rational coefficients must be distinguished from separately summing their reduced numerators or denominators. The manuscript supplies no new closed formulas for those two separate-sum sequences, only described finite checks of published prefixes.

The contribution is a self-contained written explanation connecting integration with divisibility, plus the exponent and interval extensions, not a claim to the first complete proof. The authors credit an earlier pinned Lean declaration with both original equalities for every positive n, using the finite coefficient sum and its reduced numerator. Its stated scope excludes the generalizations, coefficient corrections, and initial integrals of degrees zero and one. The described exact-arithmetic program supplies finite checks, not all-index proofs; the supplied text gives no explicit checked ranges. Beyond the separate-sum sequences lacking new formulas here, the note poses no further open conjecture.
